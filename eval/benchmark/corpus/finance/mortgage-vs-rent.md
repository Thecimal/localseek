# Buying versus renting: analysis

## Assumptions
Purchase price is $380,000 for a two-bedroom flat of similar size to the current one. The down payment is 20 percent ($76,000) taken from savings. The mortgage rate is 6.1 percent, fixed for thirty years. Current rent is $1,585 per month and is assumed to grow three percent per year. Money not spent on a down payment is invested at five percent per year before tax.

## Costs of buying
The monthly mortgage payment on $304,000 is about $1,842. Property tax adds roughly $320 per month, homeowner insurance $75, and maintenance about one percent of the purchase price per year, which is $317 per month. Closing costs are about three percent ($11,400) paid at purchase, and selling costs are about six percent of the sale price.

## Costs of renting
Rent is $1,585 today and rises three percent per year. Renter's insurance is $18 per month. The $76,000 that would have been a down payment stays invested and compounds at five percent. Moving costs are assumed at $1,200 every five years.

## Cash flow comparison
In the first year buying costs about $2,554 per month against $1,603 for renting, a gap of nearly $950 per month. That gap shrinks as rent rises while the mortgage payment stays fixed, but closing it takes years because maintenance and property tax also grow with inflation.

## Equity and appreciation
With three percent annual appreciation the flat is worth about $496,000 after nine years. Principal repayment over the same period builds roughly $52,000 of equity on top of the down payment, but selling costs consume about $29,800 of the gain.

## Risks and sensitivities
Higher interest rates, a job move before year five, or zero appreciation all push the break-even later. A mortgage rate of 5.1 percent would pull it forward by about two years. The investment return assumption is the second most important input; at seven percent, renting stays ahead for much longer.

## Non-financial factors
Buying gives stability and the freedom to renovate. Renting gives flexibility and no repair surprises. Neither is better in the abstract; it depends on how long we expect to stay in the city and how much we value being able to leave on sixty days notice.

## Taxes and transaction costs in detail
Property tax is assumed to rise two percent a year, and mortgage interest is not deductible under the current rules, so no tax relief is included in the buying figures. Agent and legal fees at purchase are folded into the closing costs above. If the flat were let out during a job move, income would be taxable, which the comparison ignores.

## Scenario table
Three scenarios were run: the base case above, a pessimistic case with zero appreciation and a 7.1 percent mortgage rate, and an optimistic case with four percent appreciation and a 5.1 percent rate. Only the optimistic case breaks even before year seven, and the pessimistic case never does within fifteen years.

## Conclusion
Taking all of the above together, the cumulative cost of buying overtakes the cumulative cost of renting only in year nine. Before then renting is cheaper; after that buying is cheaper. Since we do not expect to stay that long, the recommendation is to keep renting for now and revisit the numbers in two years.
