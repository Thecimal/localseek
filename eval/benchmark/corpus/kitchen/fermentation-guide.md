# Fermentation guide

## Basics
Vegetable fermentation relies on lactic acid bacteria that live on the vegetables themselves. Salt keeps unwanted microbes in check while the good bacteria acidify the mixture. Everything stays under the brine, in a clean jar, at a steady cool room temperature.

## Salt ratio
Use two percent salt by weight of the vegetables for sauerkraut and most mixed vegetables, and a brine of three and a half percent for whole vegetables such as carrots or green beans. Weigh the salt rather than measuring spoons, because coarse and fine salt differ a lot by volume.

## Sauerkraut
Shred the cabbage finely, mix with the salt, and massage until it releases plenty of liquid. Pack it tightly into a jar, press it below the surface and weigh it down. It is pleasantly sour after about two weeks at 18 to 20 degrees.

## Kimchi
Salt napa cabbage leaves for two hours, rinse, and coat with a paste of chilli flakes, garlic, ginger, fish sauce and spring onion. Pack into a jar leaving a hand's width of space at the top. Ferment at room temperature for two days, then move to the fridge.

## Temperature
Warmer rooms ferment faster but produce softer vegetables and less complex flavour. Below 15 degrees fermentation nearly stops. A shaded kitchen shelf is usually ideal in spring and autumn.

## Storage
Once the flavour is right, store jars in the fridge, where they keep for several months. The taste continues to deepen slowly. Always use a clean spoon so that no new microbes are introduced.

## Equipment
A wide-mouth glass jar, a fermentation weight or a small zip bag filled with brine, a cloth or airlock lid, and a kitchen scale are all you need. Avoid reactive metals such as aluminium, which the acid will attack.

## Flavourings
Caraway, juniper berries, dill seed, mustard seed and garlic go well with cabbage. For carrots try ginger and a little orange peel. Add flavourings after massaging in the salt so that they are spread evenly through the jar.

## Other vegetables
Beetroot, radishes, cauliflower and green beans all ferment well in brine. Firmer vegetables take longer, often three to four weeks, and benefit from being cut into uniform pieces so that they ferment evenly.

## Troubleshooting
Bubbles, cloudiness and a sour smell are normal. A flat, white, wrinkled film on the surface is usually kahm yeast, which is harmless; skim it off and make sure the vegetables remain under the brine. Fuzzy, raised spots in green, black or pink are mould, and the whole batch must be thrown away.
